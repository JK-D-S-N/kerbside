Model assumptions

Method

Capacity is lanes available to general traffic × saturation flow × effective green.

Delay is Webster uniform signal delay at each junction on the link, plus a BPR link delay, plus a deterministic queueing term once demand passes capacity.

Person-hours weight car delay by occupancy and bus delay by the passengers on board. Bus dwell at halts is counted as part of the bus's own free-flow time, not as congestion.

Emissions come from average link speed on a COPERT-shaped curve, so a slower journey emits more per kilometre.

Calibration. One general traffic lane at the default settings gives 990 vehicles per hour. The loops recorded 978 in the inbound offside lane at the 07:00 peak in 2023. That is corroboration, not proof: a count cannot exceed capacity, so a peak close to capacity is consistent with the model without independently verifying it.

    Three things in the raw DfI counts

    1. The bus lane is visible in the loop data, to the hour. Inbound nearside-lane flow collapses from 187 vehicles at 06:00 to 63 at 07:00, holds under 80 all day, then jumps back to 196 at 19:00. Outbound does the same, 106 to 37, then back to 311. That is the 07:00 to 19:00 restriction, written into the counts.
    2. Traffic is down on this road, not up. Inbound AADT was 16,850 in 2016 and 13,380 in 2023, about 21% lower. Outbound went 15,840 to 13,350. Covid and home working are tangled up in that, but the volume is not higher than it was before the lane changed.
    3. The morning peak has spread, which is what a capacity-constrained road looks like. Inbound flow peaks at 07:00, not 08:00, and the offside lane carries 978 vehicles in that hour, within about 1% of the modelled capacity of a single lane. People are leaving earlier to get ahead of it.

    One more thing worth knowing before the argument starts: the 2017 change made this bus lane less restrictive, not more. It ran 24 hours before 23 January 2017 and 07:00 to 19:00 after.
